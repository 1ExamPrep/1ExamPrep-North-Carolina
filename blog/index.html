Where should a new candidate start?

Start with the board, because everything else follows from it. General contracting on a project of $40,000 or more means the NCLBGC. Electrical work means the electrical board, and plumbing, HVAC, fuel piping and fire sprinkler work means the Plumbing Board. Then read North Carolina Contractor License: 15 Questions Answered for the questions most people ask, and the licensing guide for the step-by-step process. When you know your classification, its license page lists the exams, the limits and every book and course we carry for it.

  1. Pick the board by your trade.
  2. Pick the classification, such as Building, Unlimited Electrical or Heating Group 3.
  3. Check the reference list for your exam and its editions.
  4. Get the books 4 to 8 weeks before your exam date.
  5. Tab, practice and take 2 timed exams before test day.

How long should you study for a North Carolina exam?

Most candidates need 4 to 8 weeks. A simple plan that works for all three boards:

WeekFocus
1 to 2Learn the layout of each reference book; place tabs.
3 to 5Practice questions by topic; highlight the answers you look up.
6Business and Law practice for NCLBGC candidates.
7 to 82 or more full timed practice exams, then review misses.

Candidates with less time often choose highlighted and tabbed book sets or a live virtual class to shorten weeks 1 and 2.

What numbers should every North Carolina candidate know?

  • $40,000: the project cost that triggers an NCLBGC license under G.S. 87-1.
  • 70 percent: the passing score on the NCLBGC trade and Business and Law exams.
  • $17,000, $75,000 and $150,000: working capital for the Limited, Intermediate and Unlimited limitations.
  • 15 tons: the line between Heating Group 2 and Heating Group 3.
  • 90 days: the window to take a Plumbing Board exam after notice, and the wait after a failed attempt.

Before you bid near a limit or book an exam, check the date on the post and the linked statute or board page.

Which licenses do readers ask about most?

Five licenses account for most of the questions we get from North Carolina contractors. Each link goes to the license page with its exams, limits and study packages.

  • Building Contractor: The Building classification lets a North Carolina general contractor build commercial, industrial, institutional and residential buildings of any type.
  • Residential Contractor: The Residential classification covers construction and remodeling of homes that fall under the North Carolina Residential Code.
  • Unlimited Electrical Contractor (U): An Unlimited electrical license lets a contractor take any electrical project in North Carolina, regardless of value.
  • Heating Group 3 Class I: Heating Group 3 covers forced-air heating and cooling systems of 15 tons or less, and Class I allows that work in any building.
  • Plumbing Contractor Class I: A Plumbing Class I license covers plumbing systems in all structures.
